# Product thinking

Use this when the idea, audience or feature list needs focus.

Frame the job as a person in a situation trying to make progress. Write the main promise in a sentence a potential user could understand. If you can't explain it without listing features, the idea may still need work.

Map the shortest journey from first input to a useful outcome. Keep features that complete that journey or test an important assumption. Separate essentials, possible later additions and things to leave out. Include the cost of maintaining a feature, not just building it.

Compare ideas by the value they offer, their fit for the audience, what makes them different and the uncertainty involved. Don't invent demand, interviews or competitive findings. Label an assumption naturally and suggest a small way to check it.

For a palette archive, finding, inspecting and exporting a palette may be a complete first version. Accounts, feeds and recommendations can wait unless they're central to the job. A collaborative approval tool may need identity and an audit trail from the beginning.

Choose a next experiment that could change your mind: can someone complete the intended task with a small prototype? Which step confuses them? What do they actually need to save or export? A useful test reduces uncertainty rather than simply collecting compliments.

The `discovery` entry in [sources](sources.json) supports understanding users, problems and constraints before committing to a build. Prioritization advice here is a practical framework, not proof of product demand.
